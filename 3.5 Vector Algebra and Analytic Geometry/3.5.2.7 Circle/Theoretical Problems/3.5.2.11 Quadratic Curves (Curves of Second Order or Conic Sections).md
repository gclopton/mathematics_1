
Given a quadratic plane curve, the goal is to classify its type. The most direct way is to rewrite the equation so that what we measure about it doesn't depend on how the axes are placed-i.e., to express it in terms of quantities unchanged by translations and rotations. These coordinate-invariant quantities (which can be read off from the coefficients without first putting the curve in a special position) let us spot degeneracies and, just as importantly, decide whether the curve is an ellipse (with the circle as a special case), a hyperbola, or a parabola.

We are handed the general equation of a plane conic:

$$
a x^2+2 b x y+c y^2+2 d x+2 e y+f=0 .
$$


We can write this equation in matrix form. Start from the quadratic in vector form

$$
a x^2+2 b x y+c y^2+2 d x+2 e y+f=\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{ll}
x & y
\end{array}\right]}_{x^{\top}}\left[\begin{array}{ll}
a & b \\
b & c
\end{array}\right] \underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{l}
x \\
y
\end{array}\right]}_x+2 \underbrace{\left[\begin{array}{ll}
d & e
\end{array}\right] x}_{\beta^{\top}}+f,
$$

so we have

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{ll}
a & b \\
b & c
\end{array}\right], \quad \beta=\left[\begin{array}{l}
d \\
e
\end{array}\right], \quad \gamma=f, \quad x=\left[\begin{array}{l}
x \\
y
\end{array}\right],
$$

and now the equation reads

$$
x^{\top} A x+2 \beta^{\top} x+\gamma=0 .
$$


Since $A$ is real symmetric, the spectral theorem applies: there exists an orthogonal matrix $U$ with $U^{\top} A U=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\right)$, where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \in \mathbb{R}$. **Now we ask, do rotations change the eigenvalues?** If we rotate coordinates by making the change of variables $x=Q X$, where $Q$ is orthogonal ( so $Q^{\top} Q=I$ ), then the quadratic part transforms by orthogonal congruence:

$$
x^{\top} A x=(Q X)^{\top} A(Q X)=X^{\top}\left(Q^{\top} A Q\right) X .
$$

Because $Q$ is orthogonal, $Q^{\top}=Q^{-1}$. Consequently,

$$
Q^{\top} A Q=Q^{-1} A Q,
$$

so the same operation is also a (orthogonal) ==similarity transform==. Similarity preserves the spectrum; hence $A$ and $Q^{\top} A Q$ have the same eigenvalues. (_Remark_: general congruence with a non-orthogonal $P$ need not preserve eigenvalues; the preservation here hinges on orthogonality, which makes congruence coincide with similarity.)


Once we know rotations do not change the eigenvalues, any symmetric function of $\left\{\lambda_1, \lambda_2\right\}$ is rotation-invariant. So any numerical quantity that depends only on these eigenvalues––and not on the particular basis––is a candidate "rotation-invariant scalar". Now we ask, **what are the natural scalar invariants we can build from these eigenvalues and are some set of them sufficient?** By "natural," we mean basis-free quantities that depend only on the spectrum, are algebraically simple (polynomial or smooth functions of $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathbf{1}}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mathbf{2}}$ ), and can be computed directly from the coefficients without first rotating or translating the conic. When we ask "are some set of them sufficient", we are asking for a small generating set from which every other rotation-invariant scalar depending on the quadratic part can be recovered.  For any $2 \times 2$ matrix $A$, the characteristic polynomial can be written as

$$
\chi_A(\lambda)=\operatorname{det}(\lambda I-A)=\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr} A) \lambda+\operatorname{det} A .
$$


If the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_1, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_2$, then we can also write the characteristic polynomial as

$$
\chi_A(\lambda)=\left(\lambda-\lambda_1\right)\left(\lambda-\lambda_2\right)=\lambda^2-\left(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\right) \lambda+\lambda_1 \lambda_2=\lambda^2-S \lambda+\delta .
$$


Comparing coefficients gives

$$
\operatorname{tr} A=\lambda_1+\lambda_2, \quad \operatorname{det} A=\lambda_1 \lambda_2 .
$$


For $A=\left[\begin{array}{ll}a & b \\ b & c\end{array}\right]$, we have $\operatorname{tr} A=a+c$ and $\operatorname{det} A=a c-b^2$. Thus the canonical rotation invariants determined by the quadratic part are

$$
S=\operatorname{tr}(A)=\lambda_1+\lambda_2=a+c, \quad \delta=\operatorname{det}(A)=\lambda_1 \lambda_2=a c-b^2 .
$$


The pair ( $S, \delta$ ) fixes the unordered set (multiset) of eigenvalues $\left\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_1, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_2\right\}$. Concretely, $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_1, \boldsymbol{\lambda}_2$ are exactly the roots of

$$
\lambda^2-S \lambda+\delta=0
$$

so

$$
\lambda_{1,2}=\frac{S \pm \sqrt{S^2-4 \delta}}{2} .
$$


Exchanging $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ does not change $S$ or $\delta$, so ( $S, \delta$ ) recovers the spectrum up to order (including multiplicity: if $S^2-4 \delta=0$, then $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=S / 2$ ). In this sense, the elementary symmetric polynomials -the sum and the product- already encode the spectrum as a set. Consequently, by the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, every symmetric function of $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ is a function of $S$ and $\delta$.

In particular, any other rotation-invariant scalar depending only on the quadratic part (such as $\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{\mathbf{2}}\right)$, the Frobenius norm, or the spectral radius) can be expressed via $S, \delta$. For instance, for symmetric $A$,

$$
\|A\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{\top} A\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(A^2\right)=\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2=S^2-2 \delta .
$$

The spectral radius is likewise determined by $S, \delta$ through the quadratic formula above (it is $\left.\max \left\{\left|\lambda_1\right|,\left|\lambda_2\right|\right\}\right)$, though it is piecewise in $S, \delta$ rather than a single polynomial. Translations affect only the linear and constant terms, leaving $A$-and therefore $S, \delta$-unchanged.




> [!definition]
> A **similarity transform** is the change of a matrix representation of a linear map under a change of basis in the same vector space:
> 
> $$
> A \longmapsto \tilde{A} =S^{-1} A S, \quad S \text { invertible. }
> $$
> 
> That is, send $A$ to its conjugate by $S$. This captures the idea "same linear operator, different basis." Similarity preserves the spectrum (eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity), the characteristic polynomial, trace, and determinant. It does not in general preserve inertia unless additional structure (e.g., symmetry with respect to a fixed inner product and an orthonormal basis) is present.
> 
> When $Q$ is orthogonal, we have $Q^{\top}=Q^{-1}$. Thus the orthogonal congruence
> 
> $$
> A^{\prime}=Q^{\top} A Q
> $$
> 
> is simultaneously a similarity transform. In that special case, both signatures (for symmetric $\boldsymbol{A}$ ) and eigenvalues are preserved. This coincidence-congruence = similarity for orthogonal Q-is why rotations leave the eigenvalues of the quadratic part unchanged and why the invariants $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ and $\operatorname{det}(A)$ (for $2 \times$ 2) are rotation-invariant.



> [!definition]
> An **orthogonal congruence** is the transformation of a (real) matrix $\boldsymbol{A}$ by an orthogonal change of coordinates:
> 
> $$
> A \longmapsto A^{\prime}=Q^{\top} A Q, \quad Q^{\top} Q=I .
> $$
> 
> 
> It arises by substituting the change of variables $x=Q X$ into the quadratic form $x^{\top} A x$, giving
> 
> $$
> x^{\top} A x=(Q X)^{\top} A(Q X)=X^{\top}\left(Q^{\top} A Q\right) X .
> $$
> 
> 
> **_Extra Info:_** Conceptually, this is a coordinate rotation (or reflection) of the variables while keeping the geometric quadratic form the same. Orthogonal congruence preserves the rank and the inertia of a symmetric matrix (the triple: number of positive, zero, and negative eigenvalues)-this is Sylvester's law of inertia. In general, congruence (even orthogonal congruence) does not preserve the actual eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{A}$; it preserves the signature of a symmetric form, which is exactly what one needs to classify conics.




## 3.5.2.11.1 The Hessian Determinant of the Quadratic Part $\delta$

Now we can use one of these canonical rotation invariants determined by the quadratic part to tell whether a given conic is central or parabolic-and, in the central case, whether its principal curvatures have the same or opposite sign. In particular, the determinant $\delta$ is the Hessian determinant of the quadratic part. Recall that for a scalar field $f: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, the Hessian $H_f=\nabla^2 f$ captures the second-order shape of $f$ near a point: for the graph $z=f(x, y)$, at a critical point ( $\nabla f=0$ ) the eigenvalues of $H_f$ (up to a universal factor) are the principal curvatures; for an implicit level set $g(x)=0$ with $\nabla g \neq 0$, the principal curvatures are the eigenvalues of the tangential projection of $H_g$ scaled by $\|\nabla g\|$.

Looking back at our plane conic, the quadratic part is $q(x)=x^{\top} A x$, so $\nabla^2 q=2 A$ everywhere. The eigenvalues of $A$ fix the second-order shape: both positive or both negative $\Rightarrow$ elliptic (bowl-type), opposite signs $\Rightarrow$ hyperbolic (saddle-type), one zero $\Rightarrow$ parabolic. The determinant $\delta=$ det  is precisely the Hessian determinant of the quadratic part; its sign distinguishes these cases, and $\delta=0$ pinpoints the parabolic boundary. In this sense, $\delta$ serves as a single, rotation-invariant diagnostic for the curvature type of the conic near its center. If $\delta \neq 0$, the quadratic part has rank two and the conic is central: after translating to its center the linear terms vanish and the curve has two principal directions. If $\delta=0$, the quadratic part has rank one and the curve is parabolic in nature.


Let $\operatorname{rank}(A)=2$. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix this is equivalent to $\operatorname{det} A \neq 0$, hence $A$ is invertible. The stationary points of

$$
q(x):=x^{\top} A x+2 \beta^{\top} x+\gamma
$$

are the solutions of $\nabla q(x)=0$. Using $A^{\top}=A$, we have

$$
\nabla\left(x^{\top} A x\right)=\left(A+A^{\top}\right) x=2 A x, \quad \nabla\left(2 \beta^{\top} x\right)=2 \beta,
$$

so

$$
\nabla q(x)=2(A x+\beta)
$$


Because $A$ is invertible, the equation $\nabla q(x)=0$ has the unique solution

$$
x_0=-A^{-1} \beta .
$$


Now translate coordinates by $x=X+x_0$. Compute the translated polynomial step by step, keeping track of every term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q\left(X+x_0\right) & =\left(X+x_0\right)^{\top} A\left(X+x_0\right)+2 \beta^{\top}\left(X+x_0\right)+\gamma \\
& =X^{\top} A X+X^{\top} A x_0+x_0^{\top} A X+x_0^{\top} A x_0+2 \beta^{\top} X+2 \beta^{\top} x_0+\gamma .
\end{aligned}
$$


Use symmetry of $A$ to combine the mixed quadratic terms:

$$
X^{\top} A x_0+x_0^{\top} A X=2 X^{\top} A x_0 .
$$


Group the terms that are linear in $X$ :

$$
(\text { linear in } X)=2 X^{\top} A x_0+2 \beta^{\top} X=2 X^{\top}\left(A x_0+\beta\right) \text {. }
$$


By construction $A x_0+\beta=A\left(-A^{-1} \beta\right)+\beta=0$, so all linear terms vanish. What remains is

$$
q\left(X+x_0\right)=X^{\top} A X+\left(x_0^{\top} A x_0+2 \beta^{\top} x_0+\gamma\right) .
$$


Evaluate the constant in brackets using $x_0=-A^{-1} \beta$ :

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_0^{\top} A x_0+2 \beta^{\top} x_0+\gamma & =\left(-\beta^{\top} A^{-1}\right) A\left(-A^{-1} \beta\right)+2 \beta^{\top}\left(-A^{-1} \beta\right)+\gamma \\
& =\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta-2 \beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta+\gamma \\
& =\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta .
\end{aligned}
$$


Putting this together we have:

$$
q\left(X+x_0\right)=X^{\top} A X+\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right) .
$$


This identity makes the two promised facts manifest. First, the coefficient of $X$ is zero, so in the translated coordinates there are no linear terms; the curve is symmetric about the point $x_0$, which is its center (hence "central conic"). Second, the entire effect of the translation is to replace the original constant $\gamma$ by the centered constant $\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta$ (later, when we bring in , this quantity will reappear as $\Delta / \operatorname{det} A$).



After a rotation that diagonalizes  (orthogonal spectral theorem), you have two principal directions and a diagonal quadratic part $\lambda_1 X^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 Y^{\prime 2}$. The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices states that: there exists an orthogonal matrix $U$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ such that

$$
U^{\top} A U=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\right) .
$$


Rotate coordinates by $U$ via $X=U X^{\prime}$ (equivalently $X^{\prime}=U^{\top} X$ ). Because $U$ is orthogonal, this is a pure rotation. Substitute into the centered quadratic form $X^{\top} A X$ :

$$
X^{\top} A X=\left(U X^{\prime}\right)^{\top} A\left(U X^{\prime}\right)=X^{\prime \top}\left(U^{\top} A U\right) X^{\prime}=X^{\prime \top}\left[\begin{array}{cc}
\lambda_1 & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2
\end{array}\right] X^{\prime}=\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2} .
$$


Thus the rotation aligns the axes with the eigenvectors ("principal directions") and the quadratic part becomes diagonal with those eigenvalues as coefficients.

Now relate the sign pattern to $\delta=\operatorname{det} A$. In dimension two,

$$
\delta=\operatorname{det} A=\operatorname{det}\left(U^{\top} A U\right)=\operatorname{det}\left[\begin{array}{cc}
\lambda_1 & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2
\end{array}\right]=\lambda_1 \lambda_2 .
$$


If $\delta>0$, then $\lambda_1 \lambda_2>0$, so the eigenvalues have the same sign. The diagonal form $\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2}$ is then definite (both $>0$ ) or negative-definite (both $<0$ ); either way, the quadratic part is ellipse-type. If $\delta<0$, then $\lambda_1 \lambda_2<0$ and the eigenvalues have opposite signs; the diagonal form is indefinite, which is hyperbola-type. The borderline $\delta=0$ corresponds to rank one (one eigenvalue zero), i.e., the parabolic regime.


> [!NOTE]
> Two small clarifications are often useful here. First, since $\operatorname{tr} A=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, the trace helps you track whether the same-sign case is positive- or negative-definite before you rescale. Second, the "circle" is the special ellipse with $\lambda_1=\lambda_2$, which in the original coordinates reads $a=c$ and $b=0$.




At this stage you have not yet decided whether the centered conic is real vs. imaginary or degenerate-that depends on the centered constant you just produced. That is exactly what $\Delta$ will encode later.




##  3.5.2.11.3 Discriminant of the Conic

In the previous section we established that the invariant $\delta$ decides whether a conic is central or parabolic. Yet some quadratic loci fail to be genuine conics: the defining quadratic may factor into two lines (possibly coincident or parallel) or collapse to a point. In projective terms, the homogenized conic then has rank $\leq$ 2; such cases are called degenerate conics. This naturally raises the question: is there a single scalar, invariant under rotations and translations, that detects degeneracy in both the central and parabolic regimes?


### (Case $\delta \ne 0$)

When $\delta \neq 0$, there is a unique center $x_0$ that kills the linear part, obtained by solving the stationary equation

$$
\nabla\left(x^{\top} A x+2 \beta^{\top} x+\gamma\right)=2(A x+\beta)=0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x_0=-A^{-1} \beta .
$$


Set $x=X+x_0$. A short expansion (or "completing the square") gives

$$
\left(X+x_0\right)^{\top} A\left(X+x_0\right)+2 \beta^{\top}\left(X+x_0\right)+\gamma=X^{\top} A X+\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right) .
$$


Thus, after translating to the center, the conic is

$$
X^{\top} A X+\underbrace{\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right)}_{\text {centered constant }}=0 .
$$


The constant that survives, $\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta$, is itself not obviously invariant under rotations and translations when written in this "affine" form, but it has an invariant avatar that is easy to recognize once we homogenize.

Homogeneous coordinates bundle the entire polynomial-including linear and constant parts-into a single symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix. Introduce the homogeneous variable ( $x, y, 1$ ) and the conic matrix

$$
M=\left[\begin{array}{cc}
A & \beta \\
\beta^{\top} & \gamma
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{lll}
a & b & d \\
b & c & e \\
d & e & f
\end{array}\right],
$$

so that the conic becomes $[x y 1] M[x y 1]^{\top}=0$. A rigid motion is represented homogeneously by

$$
H=\left[\begin{array}{ll}
Q & t \\
0 & 1
\end{array}\right] \quad \text { with } \quad Q^{\top} Q=I
$$

and the conic matrix transforms by congruence

$$
M \longmapsto M^{\prime}=H^{-\top} M H^{-1} .
$$


Because $\operatorname{det} \boldsymbol{H}=1$ for rotations and translations, the determinant is preserved:

$$
\operatorname{det} M^{\prime}=\operatorname{det}\left(H^{-\top}\right) \operatorname{det} M \operatorname{det}\left(H^{-1}\right)=\operatorname{det} M .
$$


This yields our third quantity,

$$
\Delta=\operatorname{det} M=\operatorname{det}\left[\begin{array}{lll}
a & b & d \\
b & c & e \\
d & e & f
\end{array}\right]
$$



an invariant that detects degeneracy. In fact, $\mathbf{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}$ if and only if the conic degenerates into lines (intersecting, parallel, or double).

We begin from the centered equation (already established):

$$
X^{\top} A X+\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right)=0, \text { so } X^{\top} A X=-\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right) .
$$


Diagonalize $A$ via the orthogonal spectral theorem: choose $U$ with

$$
U^{\top} A U=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\right) .
$$


Define the rotated (principal-axes) coordinates by $X=U X^{\prime}$ (equivalently $X^{\prime}=U^{\top} X$ ). Then

$$
X^{\top} A X=\left(U X^{\prime}\right)^{\top} A\left(U X^{\prime}\right)=X^{\prime \top}\left(U^{\top} A U\right) X^{\prime}=\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2} .
$$


From the Schur-complement identity for

$$
M=\left[\begin{array}{cc}
A & \beta \\
\beta^{\top} & \gamma
\end{array}\right], \quad \Delta=\operatorname{det} M=\operatorname{det}(A)\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right)=\delta\left(\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta\right),
$$

we have

$$
\gamma-\beta^{\top} A^{-1} \beta=\frac{\Delta}{\delta}
$$


Substituting (2) and (3) into (1) yields the principal-axes form

$$
\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2}=-\frac{\Delta}{\delta} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2}+\frac{\Delta}{\delta}=0,
$$



##### Case $S>0$ (positive definite $A$ ).

Then $\lambda_1, \lambda_2>0$, so $\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2} \geq 0$ for all $X^{\prime}$. The equation

$$
\lambda_1 X_1^{\prime 2}+\lambda_2 X_2^{\prime 2}=-\frac{\Delta}{\delta}
$$

has real solutions only if the right-hand side is $\geq 0$, i.e.

$$
-\frac{\Delta}{\delta} \geq 0 \Longleftrightarrow \frac{\Delta}{\delta} \leq 0 .
$$


With $\delta>0, \operatorname{sign}(\Delta / \delta)=\operatorname{sign}(\Delta)$. Thus:
- If $\boldsymbol{\Delta}>0$, then $-\boldsymbol{\Delta} / \delta<0$ : contradiction with a nonnegative left-hand side, so no real points-this is the imaginary ellipse.
- If $\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}$, then the only solution is $\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}$ : the point ellipse (degenerate).
- If $\Delta<0$, then $-\Delta / \delta>0$ and dividing by the positive eigenvalues yields

$$
\frac{X_1^{\prime 2}}{(-\Delta / \delta) / \lambda_1}+\frac{X_2^{\prime 2}}{(-\Delta / \delta) / \lambda_2}=1
$$

a genuine real ellipse.
Equivalently, in the elliptic regime $(\delta>0)$ : real $\Longleftrightarrow \Delta \cdot S<0$; imaginary $\Longleftrightarrow \Delta \cdot S>0$; point ellipse $\Longleftrightarrow \Delta=0$.




##### Case $S<0$ (negative definite $A$ ).
Then $\lambda_1, \lambda_2<0$, so the left-hand side is $\leq 0$ for every $X^{\prime}$. Real solutions require the right-hand side to be $\leq 0$, i.e.

$$
-\frac{\Delta}{\delta} \leq 0 \Longleftrightarrow \frac{\Delta}{\delta} \geq 0 .
$$

If $\Delta / \delta>0$ (equivalently $\Delta>0$ ), we obtain a genuine ellipse (now with "negative" quadratic coefficients; multiply the equation by -1 to see the usual positive form). If $\Delta / \delta=0$, again only $X^{\prime}=0$ solves it: the degenerate point ellipse. Thus for $S<0$ the ellipse is real iff $\Delta>0$; multiplying by $S<0$ yields $\Delta . S<0$ once more.

Putting both definite cases together (remember $\delta>0$ here):

(1) $\delta>0$ and $\Delta \cdot S<0 \Longleftrightarrow$ real, non-degenerate ellipse 
(2) $\delta>0$ and $\Delta=0 \Longleftrightarrow$ point ellipse 
(3) $\delta>0$ and $\Delta \cdot S>0 \Longleftrightarrow$ imaginary ellipse

### Conclusion

What began as an investigative question-"what survives rotation and translation?"-thus singles out exactly three scalars with clean meanings. The trace $S$ and determinant $\delta$ of the quadratic part are the principal invariants of the symmetric matrix $\boldsymbol{A}$ and control rank and sign pattern; the homogeneous determinant $\Delta$ is the discriminant of the conic, invariant under all rigid motions, simultaneously detecting degeneracy and supplying the centered constant $\Delta / \delta$ for central conics. With these in hand, classification and reduction to canonical form become a short sequence of linear-algebra moves rather than a guessing game in coordinates.
