
A circle is the cleanest way geometry encodes "fixed distance." Once you see a circle as the set of all points whose distance from one point is constant, everything that follows-its equations, symmetries, and parametrizations-drops out almost mechanically. These descriptions matter because different problems privilege different coordinates: distance constraints are simplest in vector form, intersection and fitting problems prefer Cartesian polynomials, and motion/angles favor a trigonometric (parametric) form. Being fluent in all three, and in passing cleanly between them, is the practical skill.


# 1 Definition of a Circle


Let $C=\left(x_0, y_0\right)$ be a point in the plane and $R>0$. The circle of radius $R$ centered at $C$ is

$$
\mathcal{C}(C, R)=\left\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2:\|(x, y)-C\|=R\right\}
$$
where

where $\|\cdot\|$ is the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb{R}^2$. In particular,

$$
\|(x, y)-C\|=\sqrt{\left(x-x_0\right)^2+\left(y-y_0\right)^2}
$$

so the definition says: the circle is the set of all points whose Euclidean distance from the center $C=$ ( $x_0, y_0$ ) equals $R$. 



# 2. Equation of the circle in Cartesian coordinates

The Cartesian form turns a geometric locus into an algebraic object you can compute with: testing membership becomes evaluating a polynomial; intersections with lines reduce to solving quadratics; least-squares fitting becomes linear in the unknown parameters of the circle. The metric definition from §1,

$$
\left\|(x, y)-\left(x_0, y_0\right)\right\|=R
$$

already contains the Cartesian equation. Squaring both sides removes the root without changing the locus and yields

$$
\left(x-x_0\right)^2+\left(y-y_0\right)^2=R^2
$$


When the center is the origin this collapses to $x^2+y^2=R^2$; for a general center $C=\left(x_0, y_0\right)$ it is exactly the translated form above. These two equations are "foundational" in the sense that they simply restate the definition in coordinates.

## Given a general quadratic curve, when is it a circle? 


Write the conic as

$$
a x^2+2 b x y+c y^2+2 d x+2 e y+f=0
$$


The quadratic part encodes how the curve reacts to rotations of the axes. A true circle has the same curvature in every direction, so its quadratic part must be isotropic-a scalar multiple of the identity. In coordinates this means two things at once: there is no shear term $x y$ and the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ agree. Thus a necessary condition is

$$
b=0, \quad a=c,
$$

and, in fact, this condition is also sufficient (with $a=c \neq 0$ ).

To see sufficiency, divide the whole equation by $a$ and rename parameters,

$$
x^2+y^2+2 m x+2 n y+q=0, \quad m=\frac{d}{a}, \quad n=\frac{e}{a}, \quad q=\frac{f}{a} .
$$


Now complete the square in each variable:

$$
(x+m)^2+(y+n)^2=m^2+n^2-q .
$$


The right-hand side is $R^2$, so the center and radius are read off immediately,

$$
\left(x_0, y_0\right)=(-m,-n), \quad R=\sqrt{m^2+n^2-q} .
$$


If $m^2+n^2-q>0$ the curve is a genuine circle of radius $R$; if it equals zero the locus collapses to the single point ( $x_0, y_0$ ); if it is negative there are no real points and the "circle" is imaginary. These degeneracies are not afterthoughts-they are exactly what the algebra of completing the square tells you about how far the constant term $q$ sits from the squared linear terms.


It is also instructive to understand why the isotropy condition $b=0$ and $a=c$ is forced by geometry, not by algebraic convenience. If a curve is a circle, rotating coordinates by any angle must leave the set invariant. Under a rotation the mixed term $x y$ is created unless the quadratic form is already proportional to $x^2+y^2$; equivalently, the $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix of the quadratic part must be a scalar multiple of the identity. That is precisely the condition $b=0$ and $a=c$. Once this holds, translation (encoded by the linear terms) merely relocates the center; completing the square is the algebraic expression of that translation.


# 3 Parametric Representation of the Circle

The natural parameter on a circle is the angle made by the radius with the positive $x$-axis. Begin with the unit circle centered at the origin: by definition of the trigonometric functions, the point at angle $t$ has coordinates ( $\cos t, \sin t$ ). Scaling by the radius and translating by the center sends this unit point to the desired circle. Thus every point on the circle of radius $R$ centered at $C=\left(x_0, y_0\right)$ can be written

$$
x(t)=x_0+R \cos t, \quad y(t)=y_0+R \sin t, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}
$$


This is simply the composition of a rotation of the unit vector $(1,0)$, a scaling by $R$, and a translation by $C$. Substituting into the Cartesian equation confirms that the image lies on the circle:

$$
\left[x(t)-x_0\right]^2+\left[y(t)-y_0\right]^2=R^2\left(\cos ^2 t+\sin ^2 t\right)=R^2 .
$$


Conversely, for any point $(x, y)$ on the circle the vector $\left(x-x_0, y-y_0\right)$ has length $R$, so there exists an angle $t=\operatorname{atan} 2\left(y-y_0, x-x_0\right)$ with $\left(x-x_0, y-y_0\right)=R(\cos t, \sin t)$. The parameter therefore recovers every point, with the expected periodicity $t \sim t+2 \pi$ and the standard counterclockwise orientation as $t$ increases.

This representation is not merely cosmetic. Differentiating once gives the velocity field $\left(x^{\prime}(t), y^{\prime}(t)\right)=$ ( $-R \sin t, R \cos t$ ), which is everywhere orthogonal to the radius vector and has constant speed $\left|\left(x^{\prime}, y^{\prime}\right)\right|=R$. From this it follows that arc length along the circle is linear in the angle, $s\left(t_2\right)-s\left(t_1\right)= R\left(t_2-t_1\right)$, and that curvature is constant $1 / R$. In practice these features make (3.317) the preferred form for calculus and kinematics on circles (line integrals, flux by Green's theorem, uniform circular motion), while remaining algebraically compatible with the Cartesian form developed in §2.



# 4. Equation of the circle in polar coordinates

Polar coordinates are the natural language for problems where angle and radial distance are primary data. If a point on the plane is written as $P=(\rho, \varphi)$ and the center of the circle as $C=\left(\rho_0, \varphi_0\right)$, the defining metric condition "distance from $C$ equals $R$ " becomes a one-line application of the law of cosines. Consider the triangle with vertices $O$ (the pole), $C$, and $P$. The sides have lengths $|O P|=\rho,|O C|= \rho_0$, and $|C P|=R$. The angle at the pole between the rays $O C$ and $O P$ is $\varphi-\varphi_0$. By the law of cosines,

$$
R^2=\rho^2+\rho_0^2-2 \rho \rho_0 \cos \left(\varphi-\varphi_0\right)
$$

which rearranges to the compact "general-position" polar equation

$$
\rho^2-2 \rho \rho_0 \cos \left(\varphi-\varphi_0\right)+\rho_0^2=R^2 .
$$


This formula is just the distance definition in polar dress. It has two immediate virtues. First, it is rotationally transparent: only the angle difference $\varphi-\varphi_0$ appears, reflecting the fact that rotating the entire configuration by a fixed angle leaves the circle unchanged. Second, it is numerically stable to evaluate because it avoids expanding trigonometric cross terms in $\cos \varphi$ and $\sin \varphi$; if one prefers, the same equation is recovered by substituting $x=\rho \cos \varphi, y=\rho \sin \varphi$ and $x_0=\rho_0 \cos \varphi_0, y_0= \rho_0 \sin \varphi_0$ into $\left(x-x_0\right)^2+\left(y-y_0\right)^2=R^2$, then regrouping with the identity

$$
\cos \varphi \cos \varphi_0+\sin \varphi \sin \varphi_0=\cos \left(\varphi-\varphi_0\right)
$$

The special case most often used in applications is when the center lies on the polar axis. Setting $\varphi_0=0$ simplifies the general equation to

$$
\rho^2-2 \rho \rho_0 \cos \varphi+\rho_0^2=R^2
$$


If, in addition, the circle passes through the pole (so the origin is a point of the circle), then substituting $\rho=0$ shows $\rho_0^2=R^2$, hence $\rho_0=R$ for a center on the positive polar axis. The equation reduces to

$$
\rho^2-2 R \rho \cos \varphi+R^2=R^2,
$$

or, after cancelling,

$$
\rho(\rho-2 R \cos \varphi)=0
$$


The factor $\rho=0$ represents the single point at the origin; the circle itself is given by the nontrivial factor

$$
\rho=2 R \cos \varphi
$$


This is the canonical polar form for a circle of radius $R$ whose center is at ( $R, 0$ ), i.e., on the positive polar axis and tangent at the pole. It is worth noting a small but standard convention: if one insists on $\rho \geq 0$, then $\rho=2 R \cos \varphi$ traces the visible half of the circle where $\cos \varphi \geq 0$ (angles within $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ ), while the remainder is obtained by the usual polar equivalence $(\rho, \varphi) \sim(-\rho, \varphi+\pi)$. Allowing signed $\rho$ makes the formula global and single-valued.






# 5 Tangent (and Normal) to a Circle


The tangent is the local "best line" approximation to a curve; for circles it is also the boundary between moving around the circle and moving away from it. Analytically, the tangent is the line through a point of the circle that is perpendicular to the radius at that point. This perpendicularity is not an extra assumption -it falls straight out of the distance definition.

Start from the implicit equation

$$
F(x, y)=\left(x-x_0\right)^2+\left(y-y_0\right)^2-R^2=0 .
$$


Its gradient $\nabla F(x, y)=\left(2\left(x-x_0\right), 2\left(y-y_0\right)\right)$ is normal to the level set $F=0$. At a specific point $P=\left(x_1, y_1\right)$ on the circle, $\nabla F(P)=2\left(x_1-x_0, y_1-y_0\right)$ points along the radius $C P$. The tangent line at $P$ is therefore the line through $P$ orthogonal to this vector. Using the standard normal-line formula,

$$
\nabla F(P) \cdot\left((x, y)-\left(x_1, y_1\right)\right)=0
$$

we obtain

$$
\left(x_1-x_0\right)\left(x-x_1\right)+\left(y_1-y_0\right)\left(y-y_1\right)=0 .
$$


Rearranging with $\left(x_1-x_0\right)^2+\left(y_1-y_0\right)^2=R^2$ gives the compact center-point form

$$
\left(x-x_0\right)\left(x_1-x_0\right)+\left(y-y_0\right)\left(y_1-y_0\right)=R^2 .
$$


When the center is the origin this reduces to the familiar

$$
x x_1+y y_1=R^2
$$



and you can read off the slope immediately: the radius has slope $\left(y_1 / x_1\right)$ (when $x_1 \neq 0$ ), so the tangent's slope is the negative reciprocal $-x_1 / y_1$ (when $y_1 \neq 0$ ); the exceptional cases $P=( \pm R, 0)$ and $P=(0, \pm R)$ give vertical and horizontal tangents, respectively.

It is often useful to express the tangent directly from the translated implicit form of a circle. If you first rewrite §2's general circle as

$$
x^2+y^2+2 m x+2 n y+q=0
$$

then the tangent at a point $P=\left(x_1, y_1\right)$ on this circle is

$$
x x_1+y y_1+m\left(x+x_1\right)+n\left(y+y_1\right)+q=0
$$


This follows either by completing the square and applying the center-point formula above, or by the same gradient argument with $F(x, y)=x^2+y^2+2 m x+2 n y+q$ and eliminating the constant term using $F\left(x_1, y_1\right)=0$. In both derivations the geometry is the same: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius.

For completeness, the normal line at $P$ is simply the line through $P$ and the center $C$ :

$$
\left(y-y_1\right)=\frac{y_1-y_0}{x_1-x_0}\left(x-x_1\right) \quad \text { when } x_1 \neq x_0
$$


with the obvious vertical case $x=x_1$ when $x_1=x_0$. In applications, "tangent versus normal" is exactly the decomposition used in curvature computations, reflection laws in optics, and the direction of constraint forces in mechanics; on a circle that geometry is encoded algebraically by the orthogonality implicit in (3.319).

# Exercise 2: Complete the square and extract (x0, y0), R.
# Then generate three instances: circle (R^2>0), point (R^2=0), imaginary (R^2<0),
# and plot the level set(s) as requested.
#
# Plotting rules:
# - matplotlib only
# - one chart per figure
# - no explicit colors
#
# We optionally use SymPy to symbolically verify the completion-of-the-square identity.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt



def circle_params(m, n, q):
    """Return center (x0,y0), R^2, and a classification string."""
    x0, y0 = -m, -n
    R2 = m*m + n*n - q
    if R2 > 0:
        kind = "genuine circle"
    elif np.isclose(R2, 0.0):
        kind = "point (degenerate circle)"
    else:
        kind = "imaginary circle (no real points)"
    return (x0, y0), R2, kind

def F_grid(xs, ys, x0, y0, R2):
    """Compute F(x,y) = (x - x0)^2 + (y - y0)^2 - R^2 on a mesh grid."""
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys, indexing="xy")
    F = (X - x0)**2 + (Y - y0)**2 - R2
    return X, Y, F

def plot_level_set(m, n, q, title_suffix="", epsilon=None):
    """Plot the requested level set(s) for the given coefficients."""
    (x0, y0), R2, kind = circle_params(m, n, q)

    # Domain sizing
    if R2 > 0:
        R = float(np.sqrt(R2))
        pad = 1.5 * R
    else:
        # For point/imaginary, choose a modest fixed pad around the would-be center.
        pad = 2.0

    x_min, x_max = x0 - pad, x0 + pad
    y_min, y_max = y0 - pad, y0 + pad

    xs = np.linspace(x_min, x_max, 600)
    ys = np.linspace(y_min, y_max, 600)

    X, Y, F = F_grid(xs, ys, x0, y0, R2)

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))

    if R2 > 0:
        # Genuine circle: plot F=0 as a contour
        cs = ax.contour(xs, ys, F, levels=[0.0])
        # Optional label near the circle
        ax.text(x0 + 0.6*np.sqrt(R2), y0, "F=0")
    elif np.isclose(R2, 0.0):
        # Degenerate circle: single point at (x0, y0)
        ax.plot([x0], [y0], marker='o', linestyle='none')
        ax.text(x0 + 0.1, y0 + 0.1, "single point (R=0)")
    else:
        # Imaginary circle: no F=0. Plot "best-effort" F=±epsilon.
        if epsilon is None:
            epsilon = 1.05 * abs(R2)  # choose slightly above the threshold so +epsilon exists

        # F = +epsilon
        # Draw contour if it exists in the domain
        try:
            cs1 = ax.contour(xs, ys, F, levels=[epsilon])
            ax.text(x0 + 0.1, y0 + 0.1, r"F=+$\varepsilon$ (exists)")
        except Exception:
            pass

        # F = -epsilon (this will not exist for any positive epsilon if R2<0)
        # But try and catch "no contour levels found" gracefully.
        try:
            cs2 = ax.contour(xs, ys, F, levels=[-epsilon])
            ax.text(x0 - 0.9, y0 - 0.9, r"F=-$\varepsilon$")
        except Exception:
            ax.text(x0 - 1.2, y0 - 1.0, r"F=-$\varepsilon$ (no real level)")

    ax.set_aspect('equal', adjustable='box')
    ax.set_xlim(x_min, x_max); ax.set_ylim(y_min, y_max)
    ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y")

    x0s = f"{x0:.3f}".rstrip('0').rstrip('.')
    y0s = f"{y0:.3f}".rstrip('0').rstrip('.')
    if R2 >= 0:
        Rs = f"{np.sqrt(max(R2,0.0)):.3f}".rstrip('0').rstrip('.')
    else:
        Rs = f"i·{np.sqrt(abs(R2)):.3f}".rstrip('0').rstrip('.')

    ax.set_title(
        f"{title_suffix}  |  center=({x0s}, {y0s}),  R^2={R2:.3f},  R={Rs}\n"
        f"{kind}"
    )
    plt.show()
    return (x0, y0), R2, kind

    # point (degenerate): m^2 + n^2 - q = 0
    dict(m=2.0, n=-1.0, q=5.0, label="Point (degenerate): x^2+y^2+2mx+2ny+q=0"),
    # imaginary: m^2 + n^2 - q < 0
    dict(m=1.0, n=1.0, q=5.0, label="Imaginary: x^2+y^2+2mx+2ny+q=0"),
]

results = []
for case in instances:
    (x0, y0), R2, kind = plot_level_set(case["m"], case["n"], case["q"], title_suffix=case["label"])
    results.append((case["m"], case["n"], case["q"], x0, y0, R2, kind))

# Display a small text summary under the last figure
print("Summary (m, n, q -> x0, y0, R^2, kind):")
for m, n, q, x0, y0, R2, kind in results:
    print(f"({m:+.3f}, {n:+.3f}, {q:+.3f}) -> x0={x0:+.3f}, y0={y0:+.3f}, R^2={R2:+.3f}  [{kind}]")

# Exercise 3: Recognize a circle from a general conic and visualize three cases.
# - Start with ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2 + 2dx + 2ey + f = 0.
# - Test isotropy (circle) condition: b = 0 and a = c != 0.
# - Create three examples: (i) circle, (ii) ellipse (a != c), (iii) rotated ellipse (b != 0).
# - For each: plot level set F=0 and annotate A.
# - For the circle case: recover (x0, y0), R by completing the square and overlay the parametric circle.
#
# Plotting rules:
#   matplotlib only, one chart per figure, no explicit colors.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def conic_F(a, b, c, d, e, f, X, Y):
    """Evaluate F(X,Y) = a x^2 + 2 b x y + c y^2 + 2 d x + 2 e y + f on a grid."""
    return a*X*X + 2*b*X*Y + c*Y*Y + 2*d*X + 2*e*Y + f

def isotropy_test(a, b, c, tol=1e-12):
    """Return True if b=0 and a=c != 0 (within tol)."""
    return (abs(b) <= tol) and (abs(a-c) <= tol) and (abs(a) > tol)

def circle_from_conic(a, b, c, d, e, f):
    """Assuming b=0 and a=c!=0, recover (x0, y0, R)."""
    # Divide by a to reduce to x^2+y^2 + (2d/a)x + (2e/a)y + (f/a) = 0
    alpha = a
    x0 = -d / alpha
    y0 = -e / alpha
    R2 = (d*d + e*e - alpha*f) / (alpha*alpha)
    return x0, y0, R2

def plot_conic(a, b, c, d, e, f, center_hint, span_hint, title, annotate_A=True):
    """Contour F=0 on a square box centered at 'center_hint' with half-span 'span_hint'."""
    cx, cy = center_hint
    half = span_hint
    xs = np.linspace(cx-half, cx+half, 600)
    ys = np.linspace(cy-half, cy+half, 600)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys, indexing='xy')
    F = conic_F(a, b, c, d, e, f, X, Y)

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7,7))
    cs = ax.contour(xs, ys, F, levels=[0.0], zorder=1)
    ax.set_aspect('equal', adjustable='box')
    ax.set_xlim(cx-half, cx+half); ax.set_ylim(cy-half, cy+half)
    ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y")
    ax.set_title(title)

    if annotate_A:
        A_text = f"A = [[{a:.3g}, {b:.3g}], [{b:.3g}, {c:.3g}]]"
        # Place near upper-left inside the axes
        ax.text(cx - 0.95*half, cy + 0.85*half, A_text)

    return fig, ax

# Choose a clean circle: center (2, -1), R = 3, and scale alpha = 1 so a=c=1, b=0.
x0_c, y0_c, R_c = 2.0, -1.0, 3.0
alpha = 1.0
a = alpha
b = 0.0
c = alpha
d = -alpha * x0_c
e = -alpha * y0_c
f = alpha * (x0_c**2 + y0_c**2 - R_c**2)

assert isotropy_test(a, b, c)

# Recover center & R from the conic and overlay parametric circle
x0_rec, y0_rec, R2_rec = circle_from_conic(a, b, c, d, e, f)
R_rec = float(np.sqrt(max(R2_rec, 0.0)))

fig, ax = plot_conic(a, b, c, d, e, f,
                     center_hint=(x0_c, y0_c),
                     span_hint=1.5*R_c,
                     title="(i) Circle: F=0 and A annotated")
# Parametric overlay for confirmation
theta = np.linspace(0.0, 2.0*np.pi, 800, endpoint=True)
x_param = x0_rec + R_rec * np.cos(theta)
y_param = y0_rec + R_rec * np.sin(theta)
ax.plot(x_param, y_param, zorder=2, label="parametric circle")

# Numerical labels for recovered params
ax.text(x0_c - 1.4*R_c, y0_c - 1.3*R_c,
        f"Recovered: center=({x0_rec:.3g}, {y0_rec:.3g}),  R={R_rec:.3g}")
ax.legend(loc="upper right")
plt.show()

# -------------------- (ii) Ellipse (axis-aligned, a != c, b = 0) --------------------
# Construct from center & semiaxes (rx != ry) and then convert to general conic form.
x0_e1, y0_e1 = -1.0, 2.0
rx1, ry1 = 2.5, 1.2  # unequal axes
a = 1.0 / (rx1**2)
b = 0.0
c = 1.0 / (ry1**2)
d = -x0_e1 / (rx1**2)
e = -y0_e1 / (ry1**2)
f = (x0_e1**2) / (rx1**2) + (y0_e1**2) / (ry1**2) - 1.0

fig, ax = plot_conic(a, b, c, d, e, f,
                     center_hint=(x0_e1, y0_e1),
                     span_hint=1.6*max(rx1, ry1),
                     title="(ii) Ellipse (axis-aligned): F=0 and A annotated")
plt.show()

# -------------------- (iii) Rotated ellipse (b != 0) --------------------
# Build from a rotated quadratic form: Q = R^T diag(1/rx^2, 1/ry^2) R.
x0_e2, y0_e2 = 1.0, -0.5
rx2, ry2 = 3.0, 1.5
theta = np.deg2rad(30.0)  # rotation angle
Rmat = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)],
                 [np.sin(theta),  np.cos(theta)]])
D = np.diag([1.0/(rx2**2), 1.0/(ry2**2)])
Q = Rmat.T @ D @ Rmat  # 2x2

a = float(Q[0,0])
b = float(Q[0,1])
c = float(Q[1,1])
